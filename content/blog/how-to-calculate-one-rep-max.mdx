To calculate your one rep max, take a recent hard set of 10 reps or fewer and plug the weight and reps into a formula such as Epley or Brzycki. The result is an estimate of the most you could lift for a single rep, without having to test it. Your one rep max (1RM) is the heaviest weight you can lift once with good form.

## The two most common formulas

Both are described on Wikipedia's [one-repetition maximum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One-repetition_maximum) page.

- **Epley:** 1RM = weight x (1 + reps / 30)
- **Brzycki:** 1RM = weight x 36 / (37 - reps)

Use the weight you lifted and the number of good reps you completed.

## How to calculate one rep max step by step

1. **Pick a hard set** from your log, with 10 reps or fewer, done with good form.
2. **Choose one formula** and stick with it so your estimates stay comparable.
3. **Do the math.** Divide reps by 30, add 1, and multiply by the weight for Epley.
4. **Round down** to a weight you can load.
5. **Treat it as an estimate**, not a tested max.

## An example

Illustrative numbers: you bench 80 kg for 5 reps.

| Formula | Calculation | Estimated 1RM |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Epley | 80 x (1 + 5/30) | About 93 kg |
| Brzycki | 80 x 36 / 32 | 90 kg |

The two formulas give slightly different answers. Both are estimates, which is why consistency matters more than which one you pick.

## Why estimates have limits

Formulas work best with fewer reps. The higher the reps, the less accurate the estimate tends to be, which is why it helps to stay at 10 or fewer. Results also differ between exercises and between people. An estimate is a planning number. It does not mean you should try that weight.

## Estimating vs testing

Testing a true 1RM means working up to one maximal rep. It is demanding and not needed for most lifters. Estimating from a normal hard set gives you a useful number with no extra risk to your training week. If you ever test a max, use a spotter or safety arms. If a lift causes pain, stop and consult a qualified professional.

## How to use your 1RM

- Track estimated 1RM over time to see strength trends.
- Compare it with [strength standards](/blog/strength-standards) for rough context.
- Use it to plan percentage-based training if your program calls for it.

Logging every set gives you fresh estimates without extra testing. See [how to track personal records](/blog/how-to-track-personal-records) and [how to track progressive overload](/blog/how-to-track-progressive-overload).
