Strength standards are tables that estimate how much lifters at different experience levels can lift on exercises like the squat, bench press, deadlift, and overhead press. They usually group lifters into levels such as beginner, novice, intermediate, advanced, and elite, often by bodyweight and sex. They are rough guides, not targets you must hit, and your own progress over time is the more useful measure.

## What strength standards measure

Most standards compare a lift with bodyweight or show a weight for each bodyweight class. They are usually based on a one-rep max, the heaviest weight lifted once with good form. Because few people test true maxes often, many lifters use an estimate instead. See [how to calculate your one rep max](/blog/how-to-calculate-one-rep-max).

## The limits of strength standards

- **Different sources give different numbers.** Tables are built from different groups of lifters and methods.
- **They depend on how reps are judged.** A shallow squat and a deep squat are not the same lift.
- **They say little about your situation.** Training history, height, limb length, and schedule all affect your numbers.

Use them for curiosity and rough context, not as a verdict on your training.

## How to use strength standards well

1. **Define a valid rep** for each lift and keep it the same every time.
2. **Estimate conservatively** from a recent hard set rather than testing a max.
3. **Track a bodyweight ratio** if you want a simple comparison: lift weight divided by bodyweight.
4. **Compare with your past self** every few months.

## An example

An illustrative ratio check: a lifter who weighs 80 kg and squats 100 kg for one rep has a ratio of 100 divided by 80, or 1.25. Six months later they squat 115 kg at the same bodyweight, a ratio of about 1.44. Whatever any table says, the log shows clear progress. If a lift causes pain, stop and consult a qualified professional.
