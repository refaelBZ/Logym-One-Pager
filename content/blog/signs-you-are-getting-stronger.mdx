Signs you are getting stronger often appear while your heaviest single stays the same. A new personal record is a clear marker, and it is a rare one. Most weeks the evidence is smaller: one more rep, a set you used to miss, or the same weight moved with a spare rep left.

## Signs you are getting stronger before the next PR

Read the last three times you performed a lift and look for any of these:

- **Reps climbed at the same weight.** 80 kg for 5, 5, 4 last month and 5, 5, 5 this week is a real gain. [How to track personal records](/blog/how-to-track-personal-records) still matters, and so does the work between records.
- **You finish the planned sets.** Missing the last rep every week, then hitting all of them, is progress even if the load does not change.
- **You add a set you can complete.** Three solid sets become four at the same weight. That is more work you can recover from and repeat.
- **The top of a rep range arrives.** In [double progression](/blog/double-progression), filling out the reps is the step that earns the next weight.

A single easier day can be sleep, a quieter gym, or a longer warm-up. A change that repeats on the next session is the sign to trust.

## An example with no new max

Illustrative squat sets at 100 kg across four weeks:

| Week | Sets at 100 kg | What changed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 5, 5, 4 | Baseline |
| 2 | 5, 5, 5 | Third set filled in |
| 3 | 6, 5, 5 | First set gained a rep |
| 4 | 6, 6, 5 | Two sets moved |

No one-rep max was tested. The working sets still improved. If you only wrote down "100 kg" each week, this would look like a stall.

## What is not a sign

Soreness, a pump, or a session that felt dramatic does not tell you the load is going up. Neither does copying someone else's weights. Your own previous line in the log is the comparison that counts.

If every main lift is flat for several weeks, treat that as a [strength plateau](/blog/strength-plateaus) and change one training variable. If a lift causes pain, stop and consult a qualified professional.
