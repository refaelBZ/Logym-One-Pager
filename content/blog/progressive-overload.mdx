Progressive overload means making your training a little harder over time so your body keeps having a reason to adapt. In practice, you add reps or weight to your lifts gradually, and you track what you did so you know what to beat next session.

## Ways to apply progressive overload

You can make an exercise harder in several ways:

- **More reps** at the same weight.
- **More weight** for the same reps.
- **More sets** of the same work.
- **A harder variation**, such as moving from knee push-ups to full push-ups.

Most lifters rely on the first two from session to session. Change one thing at a time so you can see what worked.

## How to apply it with double progression

Double progression is a simple way to use both reps and weight:

1. Pick a rep range, for example 3 sets of 8 to 12.
2. Start with a weight you can lift for 8 clean reps on every set.
3. Each session, try to add a rep to at least one set.
4. When every set reaches 12, add the smallest weight jump available and work back up from 8.

This works well with dumbbells, where the next weight up is often a big jump.

## An example

Illustrative numbers for a dumbbell shoulder press:

| Session | Weight | Reps | Next step |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 14 kg | 9, 8, 8 | Stay |
| 2 | 14 kg | 11, 10, 9 | Stay |
| 3 | 14 kg | 12, 12, 12 | Add weight |
| 4 | 16 kg | 9, 8, 8 | Build back up |

Fewer reps after a weight increase is expected. Progress shows in the trend across sessions.

## How to track progressive overload

You can only beat last session's numbers if you know them. For each exercise, log the weight and reps of every set as you complete them, and set a target before you start. Keep your technique and range of motion the same, so heavier numbers reflect real progress. The guide on [how to track your workouts](/blog/how-to-track-your-workouts) covers the logging method step by step. If a lift causes pain, stop and consult a qualified professional.
