To track progressive overload, log the weight and reps of every set for each exercise, then compare each session with the last one on that same exercise. Before you lift, set one target to beat. After you lift, the log shows whether you hit it and what to aim for next time.

## What to record for progressive overload

You need only a few details per exercise:

- **The exercise name**, written the same way every time.
- **Weight and reps for each set**, as completed.
- **A note when something changes**, like a new machine, a different grip, or a shorter range of motion.

An optional effort rating, such as how many reps you think you had left, can show that a weight is getting easier before your reps go up.

## Set a target before every session

Progressive overload means making training a little harder over time. Your log turns that into a concrete goal for today. Pick one of these, based on last session:

1. **One more rep** on one or more sets at the same weight.
2. **A small weight increase** once you hit the top of your rep range on every set. The free [double progression chart](/tools/double-progression-chart) makes that rep-then-weight rule easy to follow.
3. **One more set**, if your program calls for adding volume.

Change one thing at a time, so your log shows what worked. The [progressive overload guide](/blog/progressive-overload) explains the options in more detail.

## Read the trend across sessions

Look at each main lift every few weeks. Any of these counts as progress:

- More reps at the same weight
- More weight for the same reps
- More sets of the same work
- The same work with a lower effort rating

A drop in reps right after a weight increase is normal. What matters is the direction across several sessions.

## An example

Illustrative numbers for a barbell row, 3 sets with a target of 8 to 10 reps:

| Session | Weight | Reps | Next target |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 50 kg | 8, 8, 8 | One more rep |
| 2 | 50 kg | 9, 9, 8 | One more rep |
| 3 | 50 kg | 10, 10, 10 | Add weight |
| 4 | 52.5 kg | 8, 8, 7 | Build reps again |

This pattern of adding reps, then weight, is called [double progression](/blog/double-progression).

## When the numbers stop moving

If a lift stays flat for several sessions, check the log before changing anything. Look for missed workouts, exercise swaps, or a change in range of motion. If training has been consistent, try a smaller weight jump or work on reps for a while. If a lift causes pain, stop and consult a qualified professional.
