To track training volume, pick one simple measure and record it every week: either the number of hard sets you do per exercise or muscle group, or volume load, which is sets x reps x weight for an exercise. Then compare the same measure week to week. You do not need to log anything new; volume comes from the sets you already record.

## Two ways to measure training volume

**Hard sets.** Count the working sets that were challenging, usually ending a few reps short of failure. This is easy to plan with, for example "10 hard sets for legs this week". Warm-up sets do not count.

**Volume load.** Multiply sets x reps x weight for one exercise. Three sets of 8 at 60 kg is 3 x 8 x 60 = 1,440 kg. It is useful for comparing the same exercise across weeks. Wikipedia's overview of [strength training](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strength_training) covers the other training variables.

## How to track training volume step by step

1. **Log every working set** with weight and reps. The guide to [what to log in a workout](/blog/what-to-log-in-a-workout) covers the basics.
2. **Choose your measure:** hard sets per muscle group, or volume load per exercise.
3. **Total it weekly.** Add up sets or volume load for each main lift or muscle group.
4. **Compare like with like.** Same exercise, same measure, week to week.

## Limits to keep in mind

Volume load mixes weight and reps, so it can rise even if the weight on the bar stays the same. It also does not show how hard the sets were. Comparing volume load between different exercises, such as a squat and a leg extension, is not meaningful. Hard-set counts are simpler but depend on judging effort honestly. Neither number tells the whole story alone, so look at them alongside your top sets.

## An example

Illustrative weekly volume for a bench press:

| Week | Sets | Volume load |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 60 kg x 8, 8, 7 | 1,380 kg |
| 2 | 60 kg x 8, 8, 8 | 1,440 kg |
| 3 | 60 kg x 8, 8, 8, 8 | 1,920 kg |

Week 3 jumped because a fourth set was added, not because the weight went up. That is why it helps to note what changed.

## When to change your volume

If you add sets, do it in small steps and keep other variables the same so you can see the effect. If your numbers start dropping across sessions, adding more volume may not be the answer; see [fatigue vs a real plateau](/blog/fatigue-vs-plateau). For tracking progress more broadly, read [how to track progressive overload](/blog/how-to-track-progressive-overload).
